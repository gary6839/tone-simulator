Why?

What it is

A passive pickup is a coil of fine wire around magnets. The moving strings change the magnetic field and induce a small voltage in the coil. Electrically it behaves like a signal source in series with a resistance (the DC resistance you can measure) and an inductance.

What changing it does

More turns of wire raise both the resistance and the inductance. You get more output, and the treble peak moves lower. That's why "hot" pickups sound thicker and midrange-heavy, while lower-wound pickups sound brighter and clearer.

The inductance, together with the cable and the pots, sets where the resonant peak sits. The resistance and the pots' load soften that peak.

About the numbers

The simulator estimates inductance from DC resistance (L ≈ R ÷ 2500, so 6k ≈ 2.4 H). That's a rule of thumb: wire gauge, magnets and coil shape all change the ratio, and humbuckers vary the most. The trend holds even when the exact numbers don't.

Try this

Sweep the pickup from 6k to 12k and watch the bump near the right of the graph slide toward the middle and the cutoff drop.

Shop tip

For an exact reading, measure DC resistance across the pickup's two leads with them disconnected from the pots. A reading far below spec can mean shorted turns; an open reading (OL) means a broken coil or lead.

What it is

The volume and tone pots are variable resistors from the signal to ground. Even wide open, each one is a load across the pickup. The tone cap sits in series with the tone pot: as you turn the tone down, its resistance drops and the cap sends treble to ground.

What changing it does

Pot value. 500k pots load the pickup less than 250k pots, so the treble peak stays sharper and brighter. That's why darker-sounding humbuckers usually get 500k and brighter single coils usually get 250k.

Tone cap. A bigger cap cuts lower frequencies as you roll the tone down. With the tone fully down, the cap and the pickup form a new peak: low and dark with .047, higher and vocal with .015 (the "woman tone").

Taper. Both pots are modeled as audio (log) taper, with 10% of their resistance at the halfway point. That's why most of the change happens in the lower half of the knob.

Try this

Roll the tone to 0, then step the cap through .015, .022 and .047 and watch the peak slide down the graph. Then set the tone back to 10 and switch between 250k and 500k.

Shop tip

Pots carry a tolerance, often ±10–20%, so a "250k" pot can measure well off its label. Measure before you install; matched pots make a guitar's controls feel consistent.

What it is

Wiring. Modern wiring connects the tone circuit to the pickup side of the volume pot. 50s wiring connects it to the volume pot's wiper, the side that goes to the output.

Treble bleed. A small network wired across the volume pot's input and wiper.

What changing it does

When you roll the volume down, the upper part of the volume pot becomes a resistance in series with the signal. With the cable's capacitance it forms a filter that cuts treble, so the guitar gets darker as it gets quieter.

50s wiring changes how the tone and volume controls interact, so less treble is lost as you roll down.

A treble bleed gives high frequencies a path around the volume pot, so they survive at lower volume.

  • Cap only: the strongest effect; can sound thin and harsh when turned well down.
  • Parallel resistor (Duncan style): evens out the effect, but changes how the volume knob sweeps.
  • Series resistor (Kinman style): keeps the knob's normal sweep and more body.

Recipes and ★. A 500k pot puts more resistance in the way, so the same cap lets more treble past it. That's why 500k pots get smaller caps. The ★ marks recipes matched to your pot value. Recipe values are typical starting points, not rules.

Try this

Set the Volume knob to about 4. Then come back to this tab and switch between None, Parallel and Series while you watch the right side of the graph.

Shop tip

You hear a bleed most when riding the volume into a clean or edge-of-breakup amp. Use the Custom recipe to match the parts you have on the bench before you solder.

What it is

A guitar cable is two conductors separated by insulation, which makes it a capacitor, typically around 30 pF per foot. It sits across the guitar's output, in parallel with everything else.

What changing it does

The pickup's inductance and the cable's capacitance form a resonant circuit, f ≈ 1 / (2π√LC). More capacitance moves the peak lower and makes it more pronounced, and treble falls away above it. A long or high-capacitance cable sounds darker, with a stronger midrange bump; a short, low-capacitance cable sounds brighter and more open.

At audio frequencies the cable is electrically short (a 5 kHz wavelength is tens of kilometers), so it acts as one lumped capacitor, not a transmission line. Its effect is large because the pickup is a high-impedance source with several henries of inductance.

Try this

Choose Typical, then slide from 3 ft to 30 ft and watch the resonant peak readout and the bump on the graph. Then try High-cap at 20 ft, roughly what an old coiled cable can be like.

Shop tip

A buffered pedal or active electronics isolates the pickup from long cable runs: only the cable before the buffer loads the pickup. Low-capacitance cable matters most between the guitar and the first pedal or the amp. A capacitance meter across tip and sleeve gives a cable's total.
